2. Rates, approximations, and numerical reliability

A treasurer values a bond that pays 4 currency units after one year, 4 after two years, and 104 after three years. The available annual zero rates are 4% at one year and 6% at three years. The two-year quote is missing. Two programs use these same observations and return prices of 94.79467720 and 94.76061267. Both programs can be arithmetically correct. Their difference comes from what they interpolate. Before interpreting the last decimal of a financial calculation, we must establish what mathematical question the program actually answers.

The cash flows and rates in this chapter are synthetic teaching inputs. They are not market quotations. The recurring object is a dated cash-flow schedule together with a rule for discounting each payment. We first specify that rule, then examine its approximation, its implementation, and its sensitivity to uncertain inputs. Every computed number is reproduced by the chapter program.

2.1 A rate belongs to a maturity and a convention Recording 4, 00:15

A cash flow is an amount paid or received on a specified date. Let $C_j$ denote the amount of payment $j$, in currency units, and let $t_j\gt0$ be its date in years from today. A receipt is positive and a payment is negative from the chosen holder's perspective. The schedule contains $J$ payments, where $J$ is a positive integer. Fixing the currency and dates is part of defining the object being valued.

A zero-coupon bond pays a single amount at maturity and nothing before then. Write $D(0,t)$ for the price today of a default-free promise to pay one currency unit at time $t$. This discount factor is dimensionless: multiplying it by a cash flow converts a future amount into its present value. Assume throughout the initial model that cash flows are certain, fractional holdings can be bought and sold, the same default-free curve applies to all payments, and transaction costs are absent.

One payment $C_j$ can be replicated by holding $C_j$ units of its maturity's unit zero-coupon bond. Adding those holdings replicates the entire schedule. In a frictionless market with a common linear price rule, the schedule's present price is therefore

\[ P=\sum_{j=1}^{J}C_jD(0,t_j). \tag{2.1} \]

This is a replication argument for additivity. Two different prices for the same replicable cash flows would permit buying the cheaper claim and selling the more expensive one, leaving identical future payments to cancel. Credit risk, collateral rules, funding restrictions, or a bid–ask spread can invalidate the common-curve assumptions. Equation (2.1) states the model under which the rest of the calculation is performed.

An effective annual zero rate $z(t)\gt-1$ represents the same discount factor by

\[ D(0,t)=(1+z(t))^{-t}. \tag{2.2} \]

To derive this representation, invest $D(0,t)$ today at an annual compound rate $z(t)$. At maturity it becomes $D(0,t)(1+z(t))^t=1$. Solving for the initial deposit gives (2.2). At a fractional year, the power convention itself is part of the model. A quoted day-count rule may assign a different year fraction to the same pair of calendar dates.

A coupon is a contractual interest payment, including one paid at maturity alongside principal. The face value is the reference principal amount used to specify the bond's coupon and redemption payments. A quoted coupon rate determines the contractual interest amount relative to that face value under the stated payment-frequency convention. It does not generally measure the current return required by the market. A yield to maturity is a single rate $y$ that solves $P=\sum_j C_j(1+y)^{-t_j}$ for a particular bond under an annual convention. It compresses the price of that cash-flow schedule into one number. A spot or zero rate instead applies to one maturity. Discounting every coupon using a quoted coupon rate, or inserting a coupon bond's yield directly as a zero rate, silently changes the valuation model.

The missing two-year rate in the running case is therefore a missing component of a zero-rate curve. It is not a missing coupon: the payment at year two is already fixed at 4. If the available observations were prices of coupon bonds, their zero rates would first have to be inferred jointly from their cash flows. That inverse problem is developed in the curve-construction chapter.

The shape of a sovereign yield curve also has a different role from this arithmetic. An upward curve alone does not identify a default premium. Expected future short rates, compensation for maturity risk, liquidity, and the instrument's conventions can all matter. Here the curve is an input to a specified price rule. Its economic interpretation requires additional evidence and a separate model.

2.2 Interpolation changes the price model Recording 4, 06:18

Linear interpolation of zero rates

Suppose a scalar function $f$ is observed at dates $a$ and $b$, with $a\lt b$. At a date $t$ between them, an affine interpolation takes the form $L(t)=\alpha+\beta t$. Endpoint matching requires $L(a)=f(a)$ and $L(b)=f(b)$. Subtracting those equations gives $\beta=(f(b)-f(a))/(b-a)$. Substitution yields

\[ L(t)=(1-\lambda)f(a)+\lambda f(b),\qquad \lambda=\frac{t-a}{b-a},\qquad 0\le\lambda\le1. \tag{2.3} \]

The weights sum to one and are nonnegative inside the interval. Thus the interpolated value lies between the endpoint values. Beyond the endpoints, one weight becomes negative. The same expression then performs extrapolation, with a different and often much less defensible error behavior.

For the treasurer, let $f=z$, $a=1$, $b=3$, and $t=2$. The midpoint weight is $1/2$, so the missing annual zero rate is $(0.04+0.06)/2=0.05$. Equations (2.1)–(2.2) now give

\[ P_{\mathrm{rate}}=\frac{4}{1.04}+ \frac{4}{1.05^2}+\frac{104}{1.06^3}=94.79467720. \tag{2.4} \]

The associated discount factors are $D(0,1)=0.961538462$, $D(0,2)=0.907029478$, and $D(0,3)=0.839619283$, rounded only for display. The program computes the price from unrounded factors. In particular, multiplying displayed rounded factors may differ in the last printed digits.

What a smoothness assumption buys

Linear interpolation has a quantitative error statement only after we impose information between the observed nodes. Suppose $f$ has a continuous second derivative on $[a,b]$. For an interior query $t$, define $k=(f(t)-L(t))/((t-a)(t-b))$ and the auxiliary function $g(s)=f(s)-L(s)-k(s-a)(s-b)$. The function $g$ vanishes at $a$, $t$, and $b$. Rolle's theorem applied twice gives a point $\xi$ between $a$ and $b$ at which $g''(\xi)=0$.

Since $L''=0$ and the quadratic factor's second derivative is 2, this equality reads $f''(\xi)-2k=0$. Substituting the definition of $k$ proves

\[ f(t)-L(t)=\frac{f''(\xi)}{2}(t-a)(t-b). \tag{2.5} \]

The three-zero construction above proves (2.5) for an interior query. At either endpoint the interpolation error is zero directly. If $|f''(s)|\le M$ throughout the interval, then $(t-a)(b-t)\le(b-a)^2/4$, with equality at the midpoint. Consequently $|f(t)-L(t)|\le M(b-a)^2/8$. ∎

The bound decreases quadratically with node spacing when the same curvature bound remains valid. It does not follow from the existence of two quotes that $M$ is small. Many sharply curved functions pass through those two observations. A spline can make a smoother-looking graph while introducing additional assumptions about the unobserved region.

Interpolation error can be propagated into a monetary error. For a positive cash flow $C$ at time $t$, its price as a function of an annual zero rate is $v(z)=C(1+z)^{-t}$. Differentiation gives $v'(z)=-tC(1+z)^{-t-1}$. If both the true and interpolated rates are at least $z_{\min}\gt-1$ and differ by at most $\eta$, the mean value theorem gives

\[ |\Delta v|\le tC(1+z_{\min})^{-t-1}\eta. \tag{2.6} \]

The date $t$ controls sensitivity as well as discounting. A tolerance stated only in rate units does not establish the monetary accuracy of every maturity's price. For several signed cash flows, applying (2.6) with $|C_j|$ and summing gives a conservative bound when the individual rate bounds are valid.

Linear interpolation of log discount factors

A second plausible method interpolates $\ell(t)=\log D(0,t)$ instead of $z(t)$. The logarithm requires positive discount factors. Applying (2.3) to $\ell$ and exponentiating gives

\[ D_{\log}(0,t)=D(0,a)^{1-\lambda}D(0,b)^\lambda. \tag{2.7} \]

This method agrees with the same endpoint prices. At year two it produces $D_{\log}(0,2)=\sqrt{D(0,1)D(0,3)}=0.898513346$. The bond price becomes $P_{\log}=94.76061267$, which is 0.03406453 below (2.4). The difference is entirely attributable to the second coupon because the endpoint discount factors are shared. Scaling every cash flow scales this difference by the same factor.

To interpret the interpolation, define the instantaneous forward rate where the discount curve is differentiable by $f_{\mathrm{fw}}(t)=-d\log D(0,t)/dt$. On a log-linear interval this derivative is constant, so

\[ f_{\mathrm{fw}}(t)=-\frac{\log D(0,b)-\log D(0,a)}{b-a}, \qquad a\lt t\lt b. \tag{2.8} \]

In this example it is 0.067793006 per year. Thus log-linear interpolation imposes a constant continuously compounded forward rate between nodes. Annual-rate interpolation imposes a different shape. Neither endpoint matching nor a small floating-point residual selects between those assumptions.

Positive discount factors are necessary for the logarithmic representation. Decreasing factors additionally correspond to nonnegative forward rates on a smooth interval, but negative rates are possible in a coherent financial model. A rising discount factor is therefore not by itself a universal arbitrage certificate. The permitted borrowing, lending, storage, and instrument trades must be stated before making that conclusion.

2.3 Equivalent compounding conventions Recording 4, 09:13

A nominal rate and an effective rate answer different questions. Let $m$ be a positive integer giving the number of compounding periods in one year. A nominal annual rate $j_m$ credits $j_m/m$ per period. Assume $1+j_m/m\gt0$. An initial unit becomes $(1+j_m/m)^m$ after a year. The effective annual rate $i$ is the actual proportional increase over that year, so

\[ 1+i=\left(1+\frac{j_m}{m}\right)^m,\qquad D(0,T)=\left(1+\frac{j_m}{m}\right)^{-mT}. \tag{2.9} \]

The second expression discounts a payment $T$ years away. When $mT$ is not an integer, using this power extends the compounding model between credit dates. A product with separate rules for accrued interest or early withdrawal needs those rules explicitly. A rate's compounding frequency alone is not its complete contract.

A continuously compounded annual rate $r$ expresses accumulation as $e^{rT}$ and discounting as $e^{-rT}$. Equivalent quotes give the same accumulation factor. Taking logarithms in (2.9), then solving in each direction, gives

\[ r=m\log\left(1+\frac{j_m}{m}\right)=\log(1+i),\qquad i=e^r-1,\qquad j_m=m\left(e^{r/m}-1\right). \tag{2.10} \]

The products $rT$ and $j_mT$ are dimensionless; annual rates are measured per year in the continuous-time representation. Equivalence means preserving these accumulation factors, rather than preserving the printed nominal number.

The continuous-compounding limit can be proved without treating a decimal expansion of $e$ as an algorithm. Hold a finite nominal annual rate $j$ fixed while increasing $m$. The mean value theorem for the logarithm gives $\log(1+u)=u/(1+\theta u)$ for some $\theta$ between zero and one, provided $1+u\gt0$. Setting $u=j/m$ gives $m\log(1+j/m)=j/(1+\theta j/m)$, which tends to $j$. Continuity of the exponential therefore proves

\[ \lim_{m\to\infty}\left(1+\frac{j}{m}\right)^{mT}=e^{jT}. \tag{2.11} \]

This limiting argument holds for every fixed real $j$ once $m$ is large enough that the base is positive, and for fixed $T\ge0$. It is a limit of a family of hypothetical accumulation rules. It does not assert that an actual bank credits interest at infinitely many instants.

The distinction also appears in asset returns. For positive prices $S_0$ and $S_1$, define the simple return $R=(S_1-S_0)/S_0$ and the log return $L=\log(S_1/S_0)=\log(1+R)$. Log returns add across consecutive dates because price ratios multiply and logarithms turn products into sums. Simple returns compound through the product of their $1+R$ factors. Neither convention changes the underlying pair of prices.

The approximation $L\approx R$ is local in return magnitude. For $|R|\le\rho\lt1$, the fundamental theorem of calculus gives $L-R=-\int_0^R u/(1+u)\,du$. Since $1+u\ge1-\rho$ throughout the integration interval, $|L-R|\le R^2/(2(1-\rho))$. A daily observation can contain a large jump. A short calendar horizon alone does not guarantee that the two returns are close. More general approximation questions require a remainder argument of the kind developed next.

2.4 Taylor polynomials need a remainder Recording 5, 00:16

A finite-order statement before an infinite series

Let $f$ be a real-valued function with $n+1$ continuous derivatives on an interval containing a base point $a$ and a query point $x$. Here $n$ is a nonnegative integer, $f^{(k)}$ denotes the $k$th derivative, and $k!$ is the factorial, with $0!=1$. The degree-$n$ Taylor polynomial matches the first $n$ derivatives at $a$. Its error is the remainder $R_n(x)$:

\[ f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k+R_n(x),\qquad R_n(x)=\frac1{n!}\int_a^x (x-s)^n f^{(n+1)}(s)\,ds. \tag{2.12} \]

The integral expression makes both the size of the displacement and the behavior of the next derivative relevant. It is the real-variable remainder recorded in NIST DLMF §1.4(vi), equations 1.4.35–1.4.37. A short derivation also shows why these particular terms appear.

The fundamental theorem of calculus gives $f(x)=f(a)+I_0$, where $I_0=\int_a^x f'(s)\,ds$. For $k\ge1$, let $I_k=\int_a^x (x-s)^k f^{(k+1)}(s)/k!\,ds$. Integration by parts gives $I_k=I_{k-1}-f^{(k)}(a)(x-a)^k/k!$: the boundary at $s=x$ vanishes, and differentiating $(x-s)^k$ lowers its degree. Rearranging and substituting successively into $I_0$ produces the finite sum in (2.12), followed by $I_n$. This establishes the formula without assuming an infinite series. ∎

If $|f^{(n+1)}(s)|\le M$ between $a$ and $x$, taking the absolute value of the oriented integral gives

\[ |R_n(x)|\le \frac{M|x-a|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}. \tag{2.13} \]

This is a statement about a specified interval and a specified derivative bound. A small displacement helps, but its meaning depends on how large the derivatives can be. Merely saying that a function is differentiable does not license an arbitrary-order polynomial, and even having derivatives of every order does not by itself prove equality with an infinite Taylor series.

Discounting exposes the relevant dimensionless size

For $f(x)=e^x$ at $a=0$, every derivative at zero is one. The polynomial is $1+x+x^2/2!+\cdots+x^n/n!$. On the interval between zero and $x$, all derivative magnitudes are bounded by $e^{|x|}$, so

\[ \left|e^x-\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{x^k}{k!}\right| \le e^{|x|}\frac{|x|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}. \tag{2.14} \]

For fixed $x$, the ratio of successive factorial terms eventually becomes smaller than any chosen constant below one. Thus the right-hand side tends to zero as $n$ grows. This extra argument establishes the exponential's infinite series; finite differentiability alone would not establish such a conclusion for a general function.

A payment discounted at a flat continuous annual rate $r$ has discount factor $e^{-rT}$. Its first-order approximation is $1-rT$. The relevant small quantity is $rT$, rather than the rate or horizon in isolation. At $r=0.08$ and $T=1$, the exact factor is 0.923116346 and the approximation is 0.920000000, a relative error of −0.337590%. At the same rate and $T=10$, the exact factor is 0.449328964 while the approximation is 0.200000000, a relative error of −55.489181%.

The approximation still matches the value and derivative at $rT=0$. That local agreement does not make it a suitable ten-year pricing formula. For $rT\gt1$, it even becomes negative. A negative approximate discount factor here diagnoses use outside the approximation's useful region; it does not describe a negative market price for a certain positive payment.

The same expansion becomes a risk calculation

For the synthetic cash-flow schedule, temporarily use one flat continuous rate $r$ for every payment. This is a separate scenario from the interpolated curve. Its price is $P(r)=\sum_j C_je^{-rt_j}$. Differentiating the finite sum term by term gives

\[ P'(r)=-\sum_j t_jC_je^{-rt_j},\qquad P''(r)=\sum_j t_j^2C_je^{-rt_j}. \tag{2.15} \]

For positive payments, the first derivative is negative and the second is positive. Increasing the discount rate lowers the price, while convexity bends the price curve upward relative to its tangent. The quantities $-P'(r)/P(r)$ and $P''(r)/P(r)$ are continuous-rate duration and convexity, measured in years and years squared. Other yield conventions change these definitions' formulas, so the convention must accompany any reported sensitivity.

Let $\Delta r$ be a rate change, in annual decimal-rate units. Taylor expansion gives $P(r+\Delta r)\approx P(r)+P'(r)\Delta r+P''(r)(\Delta r)^2/2$. At $r=0.05$, the program obtains $P=96.937896918$, $P'=-279.584505687$, and $P''=823.904982320$. A rise of ten basis points, where one basis point is 0.0001 in decimal-rate units, means $\Delta r=0.001$. The exact new price is 96.658723957; the quadratic approximation is 96.658724365.

The agreement is a check for this finite shock. It is not a proof that the quadratic formula works for every shock, nor does a single-rate shift represent every change in a maturity-dependent curve. Equation (2.12) identifies what a remainder analysis would require. For an actual curve, rates at different dates may move differently, and the sensitivity must be taken with respect to those distinct inputs.

2.5 From a series to an algorithm with a stopping contract Recording 5, 04:52

The exponential series describes a limit. An executable program must instead maintain a finite state, update it, and decide when to stop. Define the term $u_k=x^k/k!$ for an integer $k\ge0$. Since $u_0=1$ and $(k+1)!=(k+1)k!$, division of consecutive terms gives

\[ u_{k+1}=u_k\frac{x}{k+1},\qquad S_n=\sum_{k=0}^{n}u_k. \tag{2.16} \]

This recurrence reuses the previous term. It avoids recomputing a power and factorial separately at every step. The state consists of the latest term, the accumulated sum, and the degree $n$. Increasing the degree by one adds the next term. Reuse reduces arithmetic work, but says nothing by itself about the accuracy of the stopping decision.

A test such as $|u_n|\lt\tau$ measures one term against a chosen tolerance $\tau\gt0$. It is not generally a bound on the sum of all omitted terms. Even a geometric tail whose first term is smaller than $\tau$ can have total size $a/(1-\rho)$ when subsequent terms shrink by ratio $\rho\in(0,1)$. As $\rho$ approaches one, that total can be much larger than the first term $a$. A stopping rule needs information about the remaining sequence.

For a transparent teaching implementation, restrict the exponential input to $0\le x\le1/2$. After summing through $u_n$, let $a=u_{n+1}$ be the first omitted term and let $\rho=x/(n+2)$. Every later ratio is at most $\rho$, because its denominator is still larger. All terms are nonnegative. Comparison with a geometric series therefore proves

\[ 0\le e^x-S_n\le \frac{u_{n+1}}{1-x/(n+2)}=\frac{a}{1-\rho}. \tag{2.17} \]

The omitted terms obey $u_{n+1+j}\le a\rho^j$ for every integer $j\ge0$, by repeated use of (2.16). Summing gives $e^x-S_n\le a\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^j=a/(1-\rho)$. The domain restriction ensures $0\le\rho\lt1$ for every degree, including zero. ∎

Equation (2.17) supplies an absolute truncation target: stop when its right-hand side is at most the requested tolerance. It is an exact-arithmetic result. Evaluating the recurrence and the bound in floating point introduces another error source. Reporting the computed tail estimate as a certified bound on total numerical error would overstate the argument.

The following teaching function takes Python float values for its input and absolute tolerance, together with a positive integer iteration budget. A finite input outside $[0,0.5]$, a nonfinite input or tolerance, a nonpositive tolerance, or an invalid iteration budget raises ValueError. Other numeric types are outside this interface's contract. The function returns the polynomial value, the evaluated tail estimate, and the polynomial degree. It reads or writes no files. An insufficient budget raises RuntimeError. Python's accurate summation routine combines the stored terms. The complete program includes the same function and its checks.

import math


def exp_small(x, abs_tol=1e-14, max_terms=100):
    if not math.isfinite(x) or not 0 <= x <= 0.5:
        raise ValueError("x must be finite and in [0, 0.5]")
    if not math.isfinite(abs_tol) or abs_tol <= 0:
        raise ValueError("abs_tol must be finite and positive")
    if not isinstance(max_terms, int) or isinstance(max_terms, bool):
        raise ValueError("max_terms must be a positive integer")
    if max_terms <= 0:
        raise ValueError("max_terms must be a positive integer")
    terms = [1.0]
    term = 1.0
    for degree in range(max_terms):
        first_omitted = term * x / (degree + 1)
        ratio_bound = x / (degree + 2)
        tail = first_omitted / (1 - ratio_bound)
        if tail <= abs_tol:
            return math.fsum(terms), tail, degree
        term = first_omitted
        terms.append(term)
    raise RuntimeError("truncation target not reached within max_terms")

With tolerance $10^{-14}$, the run returns 1.0832870676749582 at $x=0.08$, using degree 8 and a tail estimate of $3.729\times10^{-16}$. At $x=0.5$, it returns 1.6487212707001275, using degree 13 and a tail estimate of $7.243\times10^{-16}$. Comparing against the platform's math.exp gives differences of $4.441\times10^{-16}$ and $6.661\times10^{-16}$ respectively. These are differences between numerical implementations, not errors against an exact oracle. For the same binary input $x=0.08$, an 80-digit Decimal comparison gives an error of approximately $3.634\times10^{-16}$, below the evaluated tail estimate.

A separate input illustrates why the tail estimate is not a bound on total evaluation error. At $x=0.001$ and the default tolerance, the function stops at degree 4 with evaluated tail estimate $8.335\times10^{-18}$. Its difference from an 80-digit Decimal exponential of the same binary input is approximately $4.291\times10^{-17}$. Rounding can therefore exceed the remaining truncation at a selected input. This numerical comparison illustrates the mechanism; it is not a global rounding-error certificate.

The tests additionally compare against a 70-digit Decimal evaluation of the exact binary input, test the domain boundaries, and verify that invalid inputs or too few iterations are rejected. A comparison with another numerical implementation remains a numerical check, rather than a proof that either implementation is universally correctly rounded.

The small interval is intentional. For large positive inputs, an implementation needs more terms and must handle overflow. For large negative inputs, alternating terms can be individually much larger than the tiny final result, exposing cancellation. Production exponential routines address a wider domain with methods such as range reduction and carefully controlled approximations. This function is a laboratory for recurrence and stopping logic; callers needing general exponentials should use an established numerical library and its documented domain.

2.6 Floating-point error needs a scale Recording 6, 02:01

Why the decimal input is already an approximation

A finite binary fraction is an integer divided by a power of two. Conversely, a reduced rational number has a finite binary expansion exactly when its denominator is a power of two: multiplying a finite expansion by a sufficiently large power of two produces an integer, and division by a power of two merely shifts a binary point. Thus $1/2$ and $1/4$ terminate, while $1/10$ does not. The obstacle is not that every noninteger is unrepresentable; it is that a fixed finite binary format cannot represent every rational or real input.

In the chapter's Python run, 0.1 + 0.2 == 0.3 is false. Each decimal literal is converted to a nearby representable binary number, and the addition is rounded again. Exact rational arithmetic still gives $1/10+1/5=3/10$. The failure of the floating comparison does not contradict that identity.

For the usual binary64 format with rounding to nearest, let $u=2^{-53}$ denote unit roundoff. Python's sys.float_info.epsilon is instead the gap from 1 to the next larger representable value, $2^{-52}$. The program reports approximately $1.11022302462515654\times10^{-16}$ and $2.22044604925031308\times10^{-16}$ respectively. These two conventions differ by a factor of two and should not share an unexplained “machine epsilon” label.

For a correctly rounded elementary operation whose exact result lies in the normal range, its computed value can be modeled as $\operatorname{fl}(a\mathbin{\circ}b)=(a\mathbin{\circ}b)(1+\delta)$ with $|\delta|\le u$, where $\circ$ denotes the arithmetic operation. Overflow, underflow, and subnormal outputs require separate treatment. This per-operation model does not imply that a long expression has relative error at most $u$.

Cancellation exposes earlier errors

Subtraction of nearby quantities removes their shared leading digits. If earlier calculations have already perturbed those quantities, the small difference can be dominated by the perturbations. The subtraction itself may even be exact for the two stored operands. Goldberg's discussion of cancellation distinguishes this exposure of earlier rounding from a blanket claim that subtraction is inaccurate.

The run's ordinary summation of [1e16, 1, -1e16] gives 0.0, while math.fsum gives 1.0. In a left-to-right sum, adding 1 to $10^{16}$ loses the small contribution before the final subtraction. Summation algorithms and Python versions can differ, so the chapter records the actual run rather than treating that ordinary-sum result as a language-wide invariant. The exact mathematical sum is one.

Stable evaluation sometimes requires a different formula for the same mathematical object. For $x=10^{-16}$, this run gives zero for both log(1+x) and exp(x)-1. The first loses the input in forming $1+x$; the second loses a tiny increment when the exponential is rounded near one. The specialized functions log1p(x) and expm1(x) return approximately $10^{-16}$ instead. Their intended behavior is documented in Python's logarithmic interface and exponential interface.

These functions are particularly useful in (2.10), where a small rate is first added to one or a small exponential increment is extracted. A closed-form expression is not automatically a stable algorithm. Nor does the rationality or irrationality of a constant decide which of two equivalent expressions will be more accurate.

Specify what “close enough” means

Let $a$ and $b$ be two numerical results in the same units. A mixed comparison can require $|a-b|\le\tau_{\mathrm{abs}}+\tau_{\mathrm{rel}}\max(|a|,|b|)$, where the absolute tolerance $\tau_{\mathrm{abs}}\ge0$ has the result's units and the relative tolerance $\tau_{\mathrm{rel}}\ge0$ is dimensionless. This is a chosen acceptance rule. It is not identical to every library's function named “close.” Near zero, the absolute term prevents division by an irrelevant or vanishing reference magnitude.

Exact equality remains appropriate for iteration counts, indices, and intentionally defined exact states. For an approximate price or a computed root, using equality to a target generally fails to express the intended accuracy. Replacing equality with an arbitrary inequality is insufficient: the tolerance, its units, and the failure budget all need meanings. A program can report many decimal places while its financial inputs justify very few of them.

2.7 Conditioning is a property of the question Recording 6, 06:50

A small input change can move the exact answer

The recording's two-equation example separates the accuracy of arithmetic from the sensitivity of the desired solution. Consider real unknowns $x$ and $y$ satisfying

\[ x-y=1,\qquad x-(1-\varepsilon)y=1+\delta, \qquad \varepsilon\ne0. \tag{2.18} \]

Here $\varepsilon$ measures the separation of the two line slopes, and $\delta$ is the difference between their right-hand sides. Neither symbol denotes unit roundoff or a stopping tolerance. Subtracting the first equation from the second gives $\varepsilon y=\delta$. Hence the exact solution is

\[ y=\frac{\delta}{\varepsilon},\qquad x=1+\frac{\delta}{\varepsilon}. \tag{2.19} \]

At $\varepsilon=0.01$ and $\delta=0.01$, the answer is $(x,y)=(2,1)$. Changing only the second right-hand side from 1.01 to 1.02 makes $\delta=0.02$, and the answer becomes $(3,2)$. The unknown $x$ rises by 50% and $y$ by 100%. These results hold in exact rational arithmetic. Rounding is unnecessary for the amplification to occur.

More generally, a change $\Delta\delta$ produces $\Delta y=\Delta\delta/\varepsilon$ and the same absolute change in $x$. Small $|\varepsilon|$ amplifies the effect of uncertainty in the data. Geometrically, the two lines are almost parallel; shifting one line slightly can move their intersection far along them. At $\varepsilon=0$, the question changes: equal right-hand sides give coincident lines and infinitely many solutions, while unequal right-hand sides give no solution.

A problem is ill-conditioned when small admissible input perturbations can create large output changes, under a stated choice of scales. That definition concerns a map from data to an exact answer. It does not prescribe the behavior of a particular solver. The division in (2.19) may be evaluated accurately while the resulting numbers remain poor guides to an uncertain underlying situation.

Absolute and relative sensitivities answer different questions

For a differentiable scalar map $F$ with input $s$, the leading absolute output change is $F'(s)\Delta s$. If $s\ne0$ and $F(s)\ne0$, division by input and output magnitudes gives the local relative condition number

\[ \kappa_F(s)=\left|\frac{sF'(s)}{F(s)}\right|, \qquad \frac{|\Delta F|}{|F(s)|}\approx \kappa_F(s)\frac{|\Delta s|}{|s|}. \tag{2.20} \]

The approximation is first order in the perturbation. At a zero input or output, this relative expression is inappropriate and an absolute or mixed sensitivity is needed. Changing the scales of a multivariable problem also changes numerical condition measures. A condition number should therefore accompany the variables and the perturbations to which it refers.

For $D(r)=e^{-rT}$ at a fixed maturity, $D'(r)=-TD(r)$. Thus relative rate perturbations have condition number $|r|T$ when $r\ne0$. Financial rate shocks are often specified absolutely in basis points instead. In those units, $\Delta D/D\approx-T\Delta r$. The latter formula remains useful at a zero rate. It explains why a small absolute rate uncertainty can overwhelm tiny numerical evaluation errors on a long-dated payment.

For a synthetic payment of 1,000,000 in ten years discounted at continuous rate 0.05, a one-basis-point increase gives a first-order price change of −606.53065971 currency units. Direct repricing gives −606.22749545. A solver returning its original price to the nearest cent does not resolve uncertainty in the rate at that scale.

A small residual is evidence about a nearby problem

Write a linear system as $A v=b$, where $A$ is a real matrix, $v$ its unknown vector, and $b$ its data vector. Given a computed vector $\widehat v$, define its residual as $q=b-A\widehat v$. Rearranging gives $A\widehat v=b-q$. The computed vector therefore solves exactly a system whose right-hand side differs from the supplied one by $-q$. This is the simplest fixed-matrix form of backward error: describe the input change needed to make the computed answer exact.

An algorithm is called backward stable under a specified error model when its answer solves a nearby input problem, with perturbations controlled relative to the relevant arithmetic scale and problem size. This is a property of the algorithm. When the original problem is ill-conditioned, even a nearby input problem may have a substantially different answer. The amplification in (2.19) shows why small backward error alone cannot guarantee small error relative to the solution for the original data.

Running an iterative method longer can reduce its residual until arithmetic or stopping limits intervene. It cannot identify the unknown true right-hand side more precisely. Likewise, regularization can select a less sensitive answer by changing the objective or restricting plausible solutions; that choice introduces additional assumptions or bias. It does not reconstruct information absent from the observations.

For the treasurer, a defensible report therefore combines a numerical check with a sensitivity analysis. The interpolation rule states how the unobserved curve is supplied. The rate-error budget describes what changes when that supplied curve is wrong. The arithmetic checks establish how faithfully the program evaluates the selected model. These are complementary pieces of evidence.

2.9 Limits of the calculation

The chapter establishes deterministic discounting identities, conditional interpolation and Taylor error bounds, and explicit finite examples of arithmetic and input sensitivity. It does not estimate a market curve from observed securities, establish a unique interpolation method, or infer recession or default probabilities from a yield-curve shape. Those questions require additional instruments, observations, and modeling assumptions.

The small exponential algorithm bounds truncation in exact arithmetic on its stated domain. It is not an interval-arithmetic implementation and does not certify a global floating-point error bound. The decimal comparison supports selected evaluations. The exact two-equation calculation establishes a sensitivity mechanism; it is not a substitute for the matrix analysis required for a large portfolio system.

2.10 Exercises with worked solutions

All monetary examples remain synthetic. Retain unrounded intermediate quantities and round only the requested output. The computational questions test a method or an uncertainty assumption, rather than the number of digits a calculator can display.

Exercise 2.1: equivalent quotes and an apparent improvement

Mechanism: compounding convention. A deposit advertises an effective annual return of 8%. Derive equivalent nominal semiannual and continuous annual quotes. A second advertisement says “8% nominal, compounded semiannually.” Is it the same growth rule? Explain the source of the difference and reproduce the relevant one-year amounts for a deposit of 100.

Solution

Equating one-year factors gives $(1+j_2/2)^2=1.08$. Taking the positive square root yields $j_2=2(\sqrt{1.08}-1)=0.0784609691$. The equivalent continuous quote is $r=\log(1.08)=0.0769610411$. Both accumulate 100 to 108, before display rounding.

The second advertisement holds $j_2$ at 0.08 instead. It accumulates 100 to $100(1.04)^2=108.16$, so it is a different growth rule. Its additional 0.16 is the second half-year's interest on the first half-year's interest. The advertised rates must be converted to common effective factors before comparing their numerical percentages. Fees, credit risk, and withdrawal rules would be additional comparison inputs for real contracts.

Exercise 2.2: interpolate, bound, and propagate

Mechanism: conditional interpolation error and monetary sensitivity. A different synthetic curve has annual zero rates observed at years 2 and 4. Their linear interpolation at year 3 is 5%. Assume the unknown true curve has continuous second derivative of magnitude at most 0.004 per year squared throughout this interval. Bound the error in the missing rate. For a single payment of 104 at year 3, give an exact interval of possible prices under that rate bound. Explain which part of the conclusion would fail if the curvature assumption were unsupported.

Solution

The interval width is two years. Equation (2.5) gives a rate-error bound of $0.004\times2^2/8=0.002$, or twenty basis points. The possible annual zero rate lies in $[0.048,0.052]$. The price $104/(1+z)^3$ is decreasing in $z$ because its derivative is $-3\times104/(1+z)^4\lt0$ on this interval. Therefore the exact price interval is

\[ \left[\frac{104}{1.052^3},\frac{104}{1.048^3}\right] =[89.32769339,\;90.35443850]. \]

This is an interval induced by a deterministic input bound, not a statistical confidence interval. It uses both the curvature assumption and the stipulated annual discount convention. Two endpoint observations alone provide no such curvature bound; without it, functions passing through the observed nodes can have very different midpoint values. Extra displayed price decimals cannot substitute for the missing assumption.

Exercise 2.3: exact arithmetic cannot identify uncertain data

Mechanism: conditioning and identifiability. In (2.18), hold $\varepsilon=0.01$. Suppose the second right-hand side is known only to lie in $[1.01,1.02]$, while the first equation is exact. Characterize all possible solutions. Can a solver with zero residual and arbitrary arithmetic precision guarantee the correct $y$ to within 0.1 for every input consistent with that information?

Solution

Write the uncertain right-hand side as $1+\delta$. Then $\delta\in[0.01,0.02]$. Formula (2.19) gives $y\in[1,2]$ and $x=1+y\in[2,3]$. The admissible pairs lie on that line segment; they are not every point in the rectangle formed by those coordinate intervals.

A solver given only this interval information cannot know whether the endpoint solution has $y=1$ or $y=2$. Any single estimate has distance at least one half from at least one of these endpoints: otherwise the triangle inequality would make their unit separation smaller than one. Thus a uniform error guarantee of 0.1 is impossible from the stated information. A zero residual merely establishes exactness for whichever right-hand side the solver selected. More precision addresses arithmetic error, while the unresolved uncertainty is in the input.

Exercise 2.4: design a stopping rule that means something

Mechanism: tail control and algorithmic failure. Derive (2.17) directly from the exponential-term ratios. Explain why stopping on a small first omitted term alone does not generally certify an infinite series. Modify the program's tolerance and iteration budget for $x=0.5$; design a check that distinguishes failure to meet the truncation target from floating-point stagnation. Must decreasing the requested tolerance always increase the accuracy of the returned floating-point number?

Solution

After degree $n$, the first omitted term is $a=u_{n+1}$. The following ratios are $x/(n+2)$, $x/(n+3)$, and so on, all at most $\rho=x/(n+2)$. Induction gives $u_{n+1+j}\le a\rho^j$. Summing the geometric majorant gives the bound $a/(1-\rho)$. The denominator matters: the first term of a geometric tail alone can underestimate its sum by an arbitrarily large factor as $\rho$ approaches one.

Record the degree, evaluated tail bound, and result separately for each tolerance. A too-small iteration budget should raise the documented exception instead of returning an unlabeled partial sum. The supplied program checks that behavior by asking for tolerance $10^{-30}$ with a budget of two degrees. To investigate stagnation, compare consecutive returned values while also comparing against the high-precision reference for the exact same binary input. Repeating the same rounded value does not establish a remainder bound.

A smaller tolerance can decrease exact-arithmetic truncation while leaving, or occasionally worsening, the rounding component. Agreement with math.exp measures a difference between two numerical implementations; it does not by itself give either implementation's true error. The high-precision check at $x=0.001$ illustrates total evaluation error exceeding the evaluated tail estimate. To check the effect of tolerance directly, repeat $x=0.08$ with tolerance $10^{-16}$: the degree becomes 9, the evaluated tail is approximately $2.981\times10^{-18}$, and the Decimal discrepancy is approximately $8.070\times10^{-17}$. Certification of total error would require additional rounding analysis or an appropriate interval implementation.

Exercise 2.5: a mathematically equivalent expression can preserve information

Mechanism: cancellation and representation. A continuously compounded rate is obtained from a very small effective return $i$ by $r=\log(1+i)$. Explain why computing the sum $1+i$ first can destroy information even if the logarithm routine is accurate. Give the appropriate standard-library replacement. State a useful check at $i=0$ and explain why a tiny nonzero answer is not always evidence of a bug.

Solution

The operation that forms $1+i$ rounds to a representable number near one. If it rounds to exactly one, a subsequent logarithm correctly returns zero for the wrong effective input. Information lost in the addition cannot be recovered by a more accurate logarithm of that stored one. Use math.log1p(i), which accepts the increment directly and is designed to evaluate the logarithm of one plus that increment accurately near zero.

The exact zero-input identity is $\log(1+0)=0$, so the replacement should return zero at that input. For a nonzero $i$ near zero, the derivative of $\log(1+i)$ at zero is one, so $r\approx i$ rather than zero. A small nonzero output is therefore expected. Compare against a reference for the same input using an absolute tolerance appropriate to the rate, instead of interpreting the output solely through its printed decimal length.

Exercise 2.6: specify a price report under rate uncertainty

Mechanism: sensitivity and error budgets. A positive payment of 1,000,000 is due in ten years. Its continuously compounded annual rate is quoted as 5% with uncertainty of approximately one basis point. A program returns the price to the nearest cent. Design a report that separates what the program knows numerically from what the quote supports financially. Compare the first-order and exact price effects of a one-basis-point rate increase using the chapter code.

Solution

The model is $P(r)=1{,}000{,}000e^{-10r}$, and its derivative is $P'(r)=-10P(r)$. The upward rate change is 0.0001. The program gives a first-order change of −606.53065971 currency units and an exact change of −606.22749545. Their difference measures the linearization error for this particular shock. Neither change is an arithmetic error in the original price calculation.

Report the cash flow, date, continuous convention, central rate, and meaning of the quoted uncertainty. Show an input-sensitivity interval or scenario range if the uncertainty can be specified as a bound; use a probabilistic interval only if a probability model is supplied. Report the arithmetic tolerance and its validation independently. Cent rounding can be an output-format choice, but it does not justify cent-level confidence in the economically relevant value. Additional solver iterations cannot narrow the input uncertainty.
