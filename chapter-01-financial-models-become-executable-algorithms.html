1. Financial models become executable algorithms

A treasury analyst receives a payment schedule: 100 in one year, 100 in two years, and 1,100 in three years. A spreadsheet adds the payments and reports 1,300. A program discounts them and reports 1,136.162401469 at a 5% effective annual rate. Before deciding which number answers the pricing question, the analyst must specify the contract, the meaning of the rate, and what each program computes. The central skill of computational finance is making those choices explicit enough to derive, implement, and check.

This chapter develops one certain-payment model and turns it into a reusable function. The same payment schedule provides a proof of the summation algorithm, a trace of its execution, and tests that expose missing data and inconsistent time units. The later chapters change the rate representation, introduce uncertain payoffs, solve inverse pricing problems, and choose portfolios. Those developments retain the requirement that a calculation answer a stated financial question.

Source spine: the course introduction, financial tour, algorithms discussion, and first R and Python laboratories, playlist positions 1–3 and 10–11. The worked code, invariant proof, input checks, and testing examples are teaching additions. Numerical values are reproduced in the chapter program.

1.1 Distinguish a price, an inferred input, and a decision Recording 1, 1:36

Three kinds of question recur throughout the course. In a valuation problem, the contract and model inputs are given, and the unknown is today's value. The opening payment schedule is such a problem once a discounting rule is specified. In a calibration problem, an observed price is given and a model input is chosen to reproduce it. Recovering an interest-rate parameter from a bond price reverses the direction of the calculation. In an optimization problem, the unknown is a decision, such as portfolio weights, selected to maximize an explicit objective subject to constraints.

These questions can share a pricing function without sharing an answer. A bond may have a model value at one rate, an observed trade price implying a different rate, and an allocation of zero in an investor's optimal portfolio. Calling all three calculations “find the best price” hides different inputs and different criteria. We will state which quantities are fixed and which are unknown before selecting an algorithm.

The financial objects are bonds, options, and portfolios. A bond is a debt contract specifying payments by its issuer to its holder. An option gives its holder a contractual right to a specified transaction or settlement under stated conditions; its payoff can depend on an underlying asset price. A portfolio is a collection of asset positions. The initial bond example has a prescribed payment schedule. An option introduces a payoff rule that depends on an uncertain future outcome. Portfolio choice introduces decision variables even when individual valuation rules have already been supplied.

Calculus, linear algebra, probability, and programming address different parts of this structure. Linear algebra organizes payments and exposures; calculus measures sensitivities and solves smooth optimality conditions; probability describes uncertain outcomes; algorithms specify finite operations and stopping rules. Familiarity with their notation does not by itself establish that a particular financial model is appropriate. The connection must be built from the contract and the question being asked.

The official NPTEL course record identifies Sourish Das of Chennai Mathematical Institute and Bikramaditya Datta of IIT Kanpur as instructors, with IIT Madras coordinating the offering. The recordings supply the sequence of computational problems. This study edition develops them for a reader comfortable with undergraduate mathematics and basic programming but new to financial conventions.

1.2 A payment promise is a contract, not a price guarantee Recording 2, 3:49

A bond's issuer borrows money through the contract; the holder owns its payment rights. The face value, also called principal or par value, is the reference amount to be repaid at the specified maturity. A fixed coupon rate specifies contractual interest as a fraction of that face value. The payment frequency determines how that annual interest is divided among coupon dates. The maturity date ends the contract's scheduled payments and ordinarily includes principal repayment as well as any final coupon.

For a simple example from the financial tour, face value 100 and a 7% annual coupon paid twice a year produce coupons of $100(0.07)/2=3.5$. Over two years starting immediately after a coupon date, the scheduled payments occur at years 0.5, 1, 1.5, and 2. The first three are 3.5 each; the last is $3.5+100=103.5$. The final coupon is not replaced by principal. Writing the schedule first prevents that common omission.

Face value and purchase price have different roles. Face value determines contractual amounts; purchase price is what a buyer pays at the valuation date. A buyer can pay above or below face value for the same payment promise. The opening laboratory case has face value 1,000, annual coupons of 100, and three years remaining. Its promised payments add to 1,300, but that sum combines amounts at different dates. It does not yet give their present value.

The word “promised” matters. A written schedule can be known even when payment is uncertain. Credit risk is the possibility that the issuer fails to pay the promised interest or principal as required. A fixed-rate bond can also change in resale value as market discount rates change. Investor.gov's bond overview distinguishes these credit and interest-rate risks. The model below deliberately treats payments as certain so the mechanism of discounting can be isolated.

Transferability adds another distinction. Many bonds can be sold before maturity in a secondary market, meaning a market for an already issued claim. That possibility does not guarantee an immediate buyer at the desired price. Liquidity concerns the ability to trade an amount promptly at acceptable cost. A bond's resale price need not equal its face value or its previous purchase price. A deposit's transfer and withdrawal rights depend on its particular contract and jurisdiction; a general comparison cannot assume every deposit is nontransferable or every bond is liquid.

The valuation inputs therefore include more than a product name. They must specify amounts, dates, currency, payment contingencies, and relevant contractual rights. For the initial calculation, assume certain payments, no taxes or fees, no early-redemption option, and no interim transaction. These assumptions define the object the function will value. A function can evaluate that object correctly while remaining unsuitable for a different bond contract.

1.3 Convert dated payments to a common valuation date Recording 2, 19:11

Suppose a certain investment grows one currency unit today into $1+i$ one year later, where $i\gt-1$ is a constant effective annual interest rate. “Effective annual” specifies the actual one-year growth factor, rather than a nominal quote awaiting a compounding convention. Reinvesting at the same rate for an integer number $t$ of years gives the accumulation factor $A(t)=(1+i)^t$: each additional year multiplies the previous balance by $1+i$.

For a fractional number of years, adopt $A(t)=(1+i)^t$ as the model's interpolation convention for all real $t\ge0$. An annual quote alone does not dictate how an actual contract handles fractional years; day-count and compounding rules must supply that detail. This chapter uses a flat rate, meaning the same $i$ is used for all payment dates. Chapter 2 develops more than one maturity-dependent representation.

The amount needed today to produce one certain unit at time $t$ is the reciprocal accumulation factor. Define the discount factor $D(t)$ by

\[ D(t)=\frac1{A(t)}=(1+i)^{-t}. \tag{1.1} \]

A discount factor is a unitless conversion multiplier; the discount rate $i$ is an input to its formula. Multiplying $D(t)$ by a future amount gives its present value in today's currency units. For example, depositing $100D(1)$ today grows to 100 in one year under the model. At $i=0.05$, this costs approximately 95.238095238 today.

Why does the reciprocal have a pricing interpretation?

Assume investors can both lend and borrow at the stipulated deterministic accumulation rule, can divide positions freely, and face no fees, taxes, or restrictions relevant to this comparison. Assume also that the promised future payment is certain and in the same currency. A deposit of $CD(t)$ reproduces a positive payment $C$ at time $t$. Such a position is called a replicating investment because it has the same future cash flow as the claim.

If that claim traded above $CD(t)$ and could be sold short, an investor could sell it, buy the replicating deposit, and retain the price difference today. The deposit would discharge the future payment owed on the short sale. If it traded below $CD(t)$, the investor could buy the claim and finance it by borrowing $CD(t)$ against the matching future repayment, again retaining the difference. Both strategies have no future net payment under the stated assumptions. Excluding this type of arbitrage—a gain requiring no compensating investment or risk—therefore gives the same price to the claim and its replicating position.

This argument identifies where the assumptions matter. A risky issuer's payment cannot be replicated by a certain deposit simply because its promised amount is identical. Borrowing and lending spreads, short-sale constraints, and transaction costs can also prevent the stated trades. Within the certain-payment model, however, the discount factor has an explicit economic construction rather than serving as an unexplained numerical weight.

One deposit for each payment gives the sum

Let $N$ be a nonnegative integer counting payments. For $j=1,\ldots,N$, let $C_j$ be a payment amount in currency units and $t_j\ge0$ its date in years from today. A positive amount is received by the holder; a negative amount is paid by the holder. Combining the replicating positions for all dates gives present value

\[ P=\sum_{j=1}^{N}C_jD(t_j) =\sum_{j=1}^{N}C_j(1+i)^{-t_j}. \tag{1.2} \]

The sum is linear in amounts: doubling every $C_j$ doubles $P$, and splitting a payment into two parts at the same date leaves $P$ unchanged. To see the latter directly, $(C_a+C_b)D(t)=C_aD(t)+C_bD(t)$. The payment order does not affect the mathematical sum. Floating-point evaluation can introduce small order-dependent differences, an issue treated in chapter 2.

For the opening schedule, (1.2) gives

\[ P=\frac{100}{1.05}+\frac{100}{1.05^2} +\frac{1100}{1.05^3}=1136.162401469\ldots. \tag{1.3} \]

The undiscounted 1,300 answers “how much nominal cash is scheduled altogether?” Equation (1.3) answers “what amount today reproduces the dated payments under this accumulation rule?” Both sums are defined, but only the second implements the stated present-value model. The computation assumes the contract's coupon and principal have already been mapped into the amounts correctly.

Time value is not an unconditional inequality

For $i\gt0$ and $t\gt0$, $D(t)\lt1$, so a certain positive future payment has a smaller present value than its amount. For $i=0$, $D(t)=1$. For $-1\lt i\lt0$ and $t\gt0$, $D(t)\gt1$, reversing the inequality. The program's example of 100 due in one year at an effective rate of $-1\%$ costs $100/0.99=101.010101010\ldots$ today. The reciprocal model remains internally consistent; the claim that future money must always have a smaller present value does not.

Inflation concerns purchasing power rather than the nominal discount calculation alone. If the price of a consumption basket rises, a fixed future currency amount buys less of that basket. Opportunity cost concerns the alternative return available on money held today. Default risk concerns whether the future amount arrives. These mechanisms can all matter to a financial decision, but they are different inputs. Equation (1.2) describes certain nominal payments under an assumed accumulation rule; it neither estimates future inflation nor derives a credit-risk adjustment.

1.4 Turn the sum into an algorithm and prove what the loop retains Recording 3, 8:07

An algorithm is a specified procedure for producing an output from admissible inputs. For (1.2), the mathematical input is the payment/date pairs and a rate. The output is their present value. Pseudocode expresses the procedure without committing to a particular programming language. Its purpose is to make the operations and their meaning checkable before syntax or library choices obscure them.

Inputs: N payment/date pairs (C[j], t[j]), effective annual rate i
Require: finite amounts, dates in years, t[j] ≥ 0, i > −1
total ← 0
For j = 1, …, N:
    discount ← (1 + i)^(−t[j])
    total ← total + C[j] × discount
Return total

The variable total is an accumulator: it stores the value already processed. The summation symbol in (1.2) becomes a loop that handles one payment at a time. Initializing to zero reflects the empty sum. Multiplying the cash flow by its discount factor supplies one summand. Updating the accumulator preserves all previous terms while adding the new one.

A loop invariant explains more than the final output

Let $S_k$ denote the accumulator after $k$ payments have been processed, with $0\le k\le N$. The loop invariant is the assertion that

\[ S_k=\sum_{j=1}^{k}C_j(1+i)^{-t_j}. \tag{1.4} \]

At initialization, $k=0$ and both sides are zero. Suppose the assertion holds after $k$ payments. The next update adds $C_{k+1}(1+i)^{-t_{k+1}}$, so the new accumulator equals the sum through $k+1$. Induction establishes (1.4) after every iteration. After the last iteration, $k=N$, so the returned value is (1.2). This proves the loop's mathematical result in exact arithmetic; rounding in each evaluated term and sum is a separate implementation question. ∎

A trace makes the invariant inspectable in the running case. Each row includes exactly one more payment. The final row agrees with (1.3), and the intermediate rows locate an error more precisely than a single final price would.

Reusable structure does not mean constant runtime

The loop performs $N$ discount evaluations, $N$ cash-flow multiplications, and $N$ accumulator updates. Under a unit-cost model that treats each operation on these fixed-size numbers as constant cost, its work grows proportionally to $N$. This is written $O(N)$, meaning its cost is bounded by a constant times $N$ for sufficiently large $N$. The accumulator needs constant extra storage if inputs and terms are streamed. The later implementation stores a list of discounted terms to use a more accurate summation routine, so that implementation uses $O(N)$ extra storage.

The same procedure handles ten payments or ten thousand payments without a new derivation. Its program structure is reusable; its total work still grows with the number of payments. For exact rational arithmetic, numerator and denominator sizes can grow too, so a constant-cost arithmetic model would no longer describe all costs. An algorithm's mathematical form and its computational cost need separate statements.

This summation is a direct calculation in the sense that the number of terms is specified by the input schedule, without refining a guessed answer until a stopping test succeeds. A later root solver is iterative: it repeatedly changes a guess and needs a stopping rule. Neither description establishes exact floating-point arithmetic. Even a fixed three-term sum can round.

Deterministic and stochastic describe another distinction. A deterministic procedure has no random choices once its inputs are fixed. A stochastic procedure uses random draws. An iterative root solver can be deterministic, while a Monte Carlo calculation can use a fixed number of random samples. Thus “direct versus iterative” cannot be replaced by “deterministic versus stochastic.” The course will need both distinctions to discuss convergence, runtime, and uncertainty accurately.

Generated code needs a contract that can be checked

A language model can translate a specification into code, but a plausible function name does not establish which payment schedule or rate convention it implemented. The source discussion emphasizes retaining the ability to check that translation. Here the contract, the invariant, and the trace provide independent questions to ask of generated code: did it include every pair, apply the intended discount factor, and accumulate each term once? These questions also apply to hand-written code.

A test can reveal a violation without proving all possible inputs correct. For example, a program that always returns zero satisfies scaling and permutation checks but fails the single-payment example. A single correct price can also hide an omitted term if another error offsets it. Combining a derivation, representative exact references, structural checks, and explicit failure tests makes the evidence more informative than any one of them alone.

1.5 Make the function's input contract executable Recording 11, 1:38

A reusable function needs a contract broader than one successful example and narrower than “price any bond.” The chapter function accepts two equal-length lists or tuples of finite real numerical amounts and dates, with dates nonnegative and measured in years. Its scalar rate is finite, greater than $-1$, and effective annually. Negative amounts represent obligations. Repeated dates and unordered pairs are allowed, because the finite sum does not require distinct or sorted dates. An empty payment sequence has value zero.

The function returns a floating-point present value. It does not modify the supplied lists, access a network, read external data, or write files. It raises ValueError for the checked length or numerical-domain violations. Objects outside the intended list/tuple and real-number interface can instead produce a Python type error. Overflow or nonfinite evaluated terms produces an arithmetic error; extremely small terms can underflow to zero. The implementation is intended for the moderate input scales used here, rather than certified for every finite machine input.

Download ch01_cashflows.py and run python code/ch01_cashflows.py. The implementation is shown below. It first validates the objects needed by (1.2), then computes the terms, then sums them. The code uses log1p, the natural logarithm of one plus its argument, to evaluate $(1+i)^{-t}$ as $\exp[-t\log(1+i)]$. These are mathematically equivalent for $i\gt-1$.

def present_value(cashflows, times, annual_rate):
    if len(cashflows) != len(times):
        raise ValueError("cash flows and dates must have equal lengths")
    if not math.isfinite(annual_rate) or annual_rate <= -1:
        raise ValueError("effective annual rate must be finite and > -1")
    if not all(math.isfinite(c) for c in cashflows):
        raise ValueError("cash flows must be finite")
    if not all(math.isfinite(t) and t >= 0 for t in times):
        raise ValueError("dates must be finite and nonnegative")
    exponent = math.log1p(annual_rate)
    terms = [c * math.exp(-t * exponent)
             for c, t in zip(cashflows, times, strict=True)]
    if not all(math.isfinite(term) for term in terms):
        raise ArithmeticError("present values exceed the finite range")
    return math.fsum(terms)

The full file imports math before this definition. The standard-library math documentation specifies these functions; chapter 2 examines their numerical motivation. The expression math.fsum(terms) uses a summation routine intended to reduce lost information from floating-point addition. It does not make the preceding input conversion, logarithm, exponential, or products exact.

A shape check prevents a valid computation of incomplete data

The pairing of amounts and dates is part of the financial input. Python's zip documentation specifies that the default iterator stops when its shortest input is exhausted. For cash flows [100, 1100] but dates [1], an unchecked loop would discount only the first payment and return approximately 95.238095238 at 5%. This number is a valid price for a different, truncated schedule. It is not an acceptable answer to the malformed two-payment request.

The equal-length check rejects that request before valuation. The additional strict=True expresses the same pairing requirement at iteration. Neither mechanism repairs a wrong date supplied with the correct length. For dates [1, 2], the complete two-payment value is approximately 1,092.970521542. This contrast shows why a numerical output and a successful function return are insufficient evidence that every intended payment was used.

Use the model to design tests

The chapter program compares the running price with an exact rational computation using $1.05=21/20$ and integer exponents. It checks the zero-rate sum, a payment at time zero, and the empty sum. It then scales all amounts, reverses the paired order, and splits the final payment into 500 and 600 at the same date. Each transformed schedule must have the value implied by linearity. These are metamorphic checks: they test known relationships between transformed inputs and outputs, rather than relying only on a list of isolated expected answers.

For nonnegative payments and a positive rate, the discount factors lie in $(0,1]$. If $D_{\min}$ is their minimum, termwise multiplication and summation give

\[ D_{\min}\sum_j C_j\le P\le\sum_j C_j. \tag{1.5} \]

This check can catch values with an impossible scale under the stated assumptions. It does not apply to arbitrary signed streams, and the minimum is undefined for an empty sequence. The program applies it to the nonempty positive running schedule. It also checks that the value falls when the rate rises for that fixed positive-payment stream. Chapter 3 supplies a finite derivative argument for the same mechanism under semiannual nominal quoting.

Finally, the program deliberately supplies unequal lengths, an inadmissible date, nonfinite data, and rate $-1$. Each must fail rather than return an unlabeled partial answer. The result of these executed checks appears in the code-validation record. Passing them supports the implemented examples and failure contract. The invariant proof establishes the exact-arithmetic sum; neither is a blanket guarantee that an arbitrary financial request has been modeled correctly.

1.6 Read the R and Python laboratories as implementations of one model Recording 10, 1:43

The first R and Python laboratories implement the same discounted-payment sum with different language idioms. In R, a numeric vector stores the payment amounts, another stores dates, and arithmetic computes a vector of discount factors. Elementwise multiplication produces the present-value terms, and sum reduces them to one number. The Python presentation uses a function and a loop over paired inputs. Both approaches need the same rate and date convention; the programming language does not supply those financial meanings.

The R expression sum(cashflows * (1 + rate)^(-times)) represents the same finite sum when the two vectors have matching lengths and compatible inputs. R's official arithmetic documentation describes elementwise operations and recycling shorter vectors; its sum documentation describes the reduction. A concise expression does not remove the need for input validation.

Recycling is particularly relevant to financial schedules. If a single date is supplied against two amounts, vector arithmetic can repeat that date rather than reject the mismatch. The result can then discount both amounts at a date intended for only one. Python's default zip can instead omit an unmatched amount. These are different language behaviors that violate the same intended pairing requirement. Checking equal lengths before calculation is a financial-data rule applicable to both implementations.

A library comparison tests agreement at a stated input

The laboratories also approximate $e^x$ with a finite sum. With nonnegative integer degree $n$, the polynomial is $\sum_{k=0}^{n}x^k/k!$, where $0!=1$ and $k!$ is the product of the positive integers through $k$ for $k\ge1$. Its terms can be generated recursively: starting with $u_0=1$, use $u_{k+1}=u_kx/(k+1)$. This avoids recomputing each power and factorial. Chapter 2 derives the approximation and a bounded-domain tail test; the recurrence alone is not an error guarantee.

At the laboratory input $x=2$, the companion's degree-10 sum is approximately 7.388994708995, differing from math.exp(2) by $-6.138993594\times10^{-5}$. At degree 20 it is approximately 7.389056098931, with library discrepancy $-4.529709940\times10^{-14}$. This comparison shows increased agreement for those two degrees. It does not establish that a last-term tolerance equals a count of correct decimal places, nor that either numerical result is an exact oracle.

1.7 Reproduce the calculation before comparing its speed Recording 11, 10:14

A notebook separates a calculation into cells while retaining variables in a shared running session. That arrangement supports experimentation, but the visible order of cells need not be the order in which they were executed. A later cell can use an old value even after an earlier input cell has been edited. A successful output in one session therefore does not establish that the saved notebook can recreate it from a fresh session.

The source Python laboratory illustrates a missing dependency: a table cell uses the principal before the defining cell has run, producing a name error. The chapter program reproduces the mechanism in an empty namespace with the statement amount = principal * 1.08. Because no value for principal exists, Python raises NameError. Running the definition first repairs that missing dependency. It does not validate the intended principal amount or rate convention.

A reproducible calculation starts from a fresh session, executes definitions and inputs before their uses, and runs all cells in their intended order. Keep the payment schedule and its units next to the function call, retain the software version, and save the output used in the report. A hosted notebook can reduce installation work, but its runtime and packages are still part of the computational environment. The repository's standard-library companion makes this first laboratory reproducible without relying on a particular notebook interface.

Printed agreement and numerical closeness answer different questions

The R and Python laboratories finish by testing the equality of decimal floating-point expressions. In the local Python run, 0.1 + 0.2 == 0.3 returns false. The decimal literals are represented by nearby binary floating-point values, and addition rounds again. Chapter 2 derives the representation issue and explains why this observation does not contradict the rational identity $1/10+1/5=3/10$.

For finite numerical results $a$ and $b$, one possible closeness rule uses a nonnegative absolute tolerance $\varepsilon_a$ and relative tolerance $\varepsilon_r$:

\[ |a-b|\le\max\!\left(\varepsilon_a, \varepsilon_r\max(|a|,|b|)\right). \tag{1.6} \]

The absolute tolerance has the same units as the compared output. The relative tolerance is dimensionless and scales with its magnitude. Python's math.isclose implements this kind of rule. With relative tolerance $10^{-15}$, the two expressions in the preceding example pass the chapter's check. That is an explicitly chosen numerical comparison, not a proof that any two prices within that tolerance are economically interchangeable.

Near zero, a relative tolerance alone can demand an unhelpfully tiny absolute difference. A cash-flow residual may need an absolute tolerance stated in currency units. Conversely, the same absolute tolerance can be too permissive for a very small rate. Replacing equality with “greater than or equal” supplies only a one-sided ordering and does not implement closeness. Tolerances must correspond to the quantity and the question, rather than being selected to make a failing test pass.

Measure the same operation repeatedly

The laboratories compare custom exponential code with built-in functions and connect small per-call costs to repeated pricing work. A single observed duration can include timer overhead, process scheduling, notebook initialization, or output formatting. It cannot by itself establish a portable speed ratio between algorithms. Matching printed decimal results also does not establish that the two routines provide the same accuracy or domain coverage.

The companion has an optional timing experiment: run python code/ch01_cashflows.py --benchmark. After the correctness checks, it times five batches of 10,000 calls to the validated three-payment pricing function and reports each batch divided by its call count. This normalization produces time per function call. The benchmark includes domain checks and function-call overhead, and excludes printing from the timed operation. It measures the complete stated function on those inputs; comparing it with an exponent-only timer would compare different work.

Python's timeit documentation explains repeated timing and cautions against interpreting interference from other processes as intrinsic variability of the snippet. Inspect the full set of batch results and retain the environment. Use timing to investigate a correctly specified workload, then check whether a proposed optimization preserves the necessary contract and accuracy. Removing validation can lower a duration while changing what the function promises.

The first laboratory now has three separate records: the financial inputs and assumptions, evidence that the implementation evaluates the intended model on tested cases, and any measured runtime under a stated environment. Combining them into one label such as “exact and fast” would discard the distinctions needed to assess the calculation.

1.8 Limits of the first pricing function

The function evaluates a supplied certain-payment schedule at a supplied flat effective annual rate. It does not identify that rate from market instruments, estimate default probabilities, account for accrued interest or irregular calendar conventions, or price a contingent payoff from its probability distribution. Those are changes to the financial problem, rather than missing decimal places in the sum.

The exact-arithmetic invariant proves what the summation procedure computes. The execution checks support selected numerical behavior and input failures. Neither result validates all possible floating-point magnitudes or proves that the payment data match a real contract. The source notebooks motivate experiments; the companion records a reproducible interpretation with explicit units and environment.

The remaining course develops the missing layers in a controlled order. Rate conventions and numerical error come next, followed by constrained portfolio choice and matrix methods. Options introduce state-dependent payments before replication, calibration, differential-equation methods, and Monte Carlo estimation. The present function remains useful as a simple reference: every extension should state which object, assumption, or algorithm it changes.

1.9 Exercises with worked solutions

The numerical inputs remain teaching examples. State the contract and interpretation before choosing a formula. Use the supplied program to reproduce the computed values, but show why each check is relevant.

Exercise 1.1: distinguish contract amounts from valuation

Mechanism: payment mapping. A bond has face value 1,000, annual coupons of 100, and three years remaining immediately after a coupon date. Write its complete payment schedule. At an effective annual rate of 5%, explain why neither 1,000 nor the sum 1,300 is generally its present value. Show how the correct price is assembled.

Solution

The dates are years 1, 2, and 3. The first two payments are coupons of 100. The final payment is coupon plus principal, $100+1000=1100$. Face value is the principal component due at maturity; it is not a pricing instruction. The sum 1,300 records the total undiscounted payments without their timing.

Under the stated flat accumulation rule, discount each amount at its own date. The three present values are approximately 95.238095238, 90.702947846, and 950.221358385, summing to 1,136.162401469. The trace in Table 1.1 supplies the same calculation term by term. Because the annual coupon payment exceeds the interest on face value at the stipulated 5% rate, a price above face is compatible with the model. No statement about issuer credit quality follows from that comparison.

Exercise 1.2: identify the assumption behind “future money is worth less”

Mechanism: a limiting case and a counterexample. For one certain payment of 100 due in a year, derive its present value at effective annual rate $i\gt-1$. Determine exactly when the value is below, equal to, or above 100. Reproduce the result at $i=-0.01$, and explain why inflation is not required to perform this calculation.

Solution

To reproduce 100 after one year, deposit $100/(1+i)$ today. The denominator is positive on the stated domain. It exceeds one for $i\gt0$, equals one at zero, and lies between zero and one for negative admissible rates. Division therefore gives a value below, equal to, or above 100 in those three cases respectively.

At $i=-0.01$, the amount is $100/0.99=101.010101010\ldots$. The chapter program checks this counterexample. It follows from the stipulated nominal accumulation rule, without an inflation forecast. Purchasing-power comparisons would additionally require a model or observation of future goods prices. Conflating those questions would obscure which input generated the present-value result.

Exercise 1.3: prove the loop and assess its scaling

Mechanism: an invariant and a cost model. State what the accumulator must equal after $k$ iterations of the cash-flow loop. Prove it for every $k$ from zero through $N$. Explain how an omitted initialization or a reset inside the loop would violate the argument. Does using one function for any schedule imply a runtime independent of $N$?

Solution

The assertion is $S_k=\sum_{j=1}^k C_j(1+i)^{-t_j}$. At zero iterations the sum is empty, so it equals zero; this requires initializing the accumulator to zero. If the assertion holds through $k$, adding the discounted payment numbered $k+1$ produces the sum through $k+1$. Induction gives the complete present value at $k=N$.

Without initialization the variable may be undefined or retain an unintended earlier state. Resetting it to zero inside each iteration erases previous terms, so the inductive step no longer has the required prior sum. The final value would then contain only the last term. Checking only a one-payment schedule would fail to reveal that particular reset bug.

The function's logic is reusable across schedule lengths, but it still evaluates one term per payment. Under the fixed-size unit-cost arithmetic model, work is $O(N)$. Streaming can keep extra storage constant; constructing all discounted terms before summation uses linear extra storage. Neither statement supplies a machine-independent timing constant.

Exercise 1.4: diagnose a shape error before valuing it

Mechanism: input pairing. The requested amounts are [100,1100] but the only supplied date is [1]. Explain what Python's default zip would do and why returning its sum is inappropriate. What does strict pairing establish, and what does it leave unchecked if both dates are supplied?

Solution

The default iterator emits only the pair (100,1) and stops when the date list ends. A summation built on it would omit the 1,100 payment and return approximately 95.238095238 at 5%. The request lacks enough dates to define the intended valuation, so the function should reject it rather than guess the missing date or silently discard a payment.

An equal-length check and strict pairing establish that each supplied amount has a supplied date. They do not establish that those dates are correct. If the completed dates are years 1 and 2, the value is approximately 1,092.970521542. Dates in months entered as years would still pass a length check and define a different calculation. Numerical-domain checks, unit declarations, and contract verification therefore remain necessary after shape validation.

Exercise 1.5: a transformed-input test can pass for the wrong model

Mechanism: limits of verification evidence. Suppose a faulty implementation ignores the supplied rate and always discounts at 5%. Would scaling all amounts, reversing the paired order, and splitting a payment necessarily expose the error? Design a check that would detect it in the running positive-payment case.

Solution

At a fixed internally used rate, the faulty function is still linear in cash amounts and invariant under payment reordering and splitting. It can pass all three transformations. It can even reproduce the reference price when the requested rate happens to be 5%. Those successes establish relationships the wrong function shares with the correct one, rather than proving it uses every intended input.

Change the requested rate while holding amounts and dates fixed. At rates 2% and 8%, the correct running prices are approximately 1,230.710661812 and 1,051.541939745. A function that returns the 5% value for both violates these references and the strictly decreasing rate response for positive amounts at positive dates. Combine such checks with the zero-rate undiscounted sum and the derivation. Every finite test suite has a scope; choosing structurally different checks improves its ability to find plausible defects.

Exercise 1.6: design a reproducible performance comparison

Mechanism: comparison under equal requirements. One notebook cell times a single validated bond-price call; another times an exponent evaluation. The shorter duration is advertised as the faster pricing algorithm. Explain the defect and specify a defensible experiment. Must two programs that print the same rounded price have the same accuracy?

Solution

The measured operations perform different work. The first handles a schedule, checks inputs, evaluates multiple discounts, and sums terms. The second evaluates one elementary function. Its shorter duration does not compare complete pricing methods. A single call can also be dominated by timer overhead or session effects.

Specify the same schedules, rate conventions, accepted input domains, validation requirements, and output-error criteria for both implementations. Check their results before timing. Initialize inputs outside the timed region, avoid printing inside it, execute repeated batches, divide each batch duration by its call count, and retain the results and software environment. Start notebooks from a fresh session so stale variables do not select different inputs.

Rounded output can conceal different underlying values, and two approximations can agree while sharing a modeling error. Accuracy requires a stated reference or error argument and a suitable scale. The exponential comparison in §1.6 measures agreement with a library result at selected degrees; it does not equate timing, printed digits, and exact mathematical error. A defensible performance conclusion states which common requirements were satisfied and which workload was measured.
